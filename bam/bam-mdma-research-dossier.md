## 1. BAM/BAD Pathophysiology & Diagnosis

**[VERIFIED]** The SeHCAT (75-selenium homocholic acid taurine) test is considered the gold-standard nuclear medicine test for diagnosing bile acid malabsorption, providing a quantitative severity assessment that can predict response to bile acid sequestrant therapy.
> "The 75-selenium homocholic acid taurine (SeHCAT) test... is considered to be the gold standard for the diagnosis of bile acid malabsorption (BAM)... it provides a quantitative assessment to estimate the severity of BAM and the possible response to therapy with bile acid sequestrants (BASs)."

> "there is no general agreement regarding its cutoff value and the test is not widely available"

**[VERIFIED]** SeHCAT measures whole-body retention of the radiolabelled bile acid analogue at 7 days post-dose; a retention value of ≤10–15% is typically used as the diagnostic cutoff.
> "measures the whole body retention of a radiolabelled taurine-conjugated bile acid analogue (75Se) after seven days; a retention value of ≤10–15% is usually considered diagnostic"
Source: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1155/2018/2097359 (vote 3-0)

**[VERIFIED]** Bile acid malabsorption/diarrhea can arise from both organic and functional disorders and clinically overlaps with diarrhea-predominant IBS (IBS-D) — directly relevant to why differential diagnosis is hard.
> "bile acid malabsorption (BAM) causing chronic diarrhea, which may be due to organic as well as functional disorders, including diarrheic-type irritable bowel syndrome (IBS-D)"

**[USER-PROVIDED PDF — VERIFIED]** In BAD, excess colonic bile acids activate colonocyte/EC-cell TGR5, driving motility, secretion, mucosal permeability, and visceral sensitization that together produce diarrhea.
> "Upon reaching the colonic lumen, primary or secondary bile acids activate TGR5 on the cell membrane of colonocytes, promoting colonic motility, fluid, and electrolyte secretion, increased mucosal permeability, and visceral sensitization that ultimately leads to diarrhea."
Source: Yang et al., "Bile Acid Diarrhea: From Molecular Mechanisms to Clinical Diagnosis and Treatment in the Era of Precision Medicine," *Int J Mol Sci* 2024, 25(3):1544 — https://www.mdpi.com/1422-0067/25/3/1544 / https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10855108/ — **you provided this PDF directly; confirmed by full-text read.**

**[USER-PROVIDED PDF — VERIFIED, new]** BAD has three recognized subtypes: **Type 1** — terminal ileal disease (Crohn's, resection) or radiation injury impairing bile acid reabsorption; **Type 2** — idiopathic/primary; **Type 3** — other conditions (celiac disease, cholecystectomy) that alter intestinal bile acid absorption. (You mentioned going through elimination to reach a BAM diagnosis — this typology is the standard framework your gastroenterologist would have been working from; without more detail it's not possible to say from this dossier which subtype applies to you.)

**[USER-PROVIDED PDF — VERIFIED, new — a 4th subtype exists in the more recent (2024) literature]** Di Ciaula et al. 2024 (*Eur J Intern Med*, a more recent and more heavily-referenced review than Yang 2024) describes a **4th subtype**: **Type IV — excessive bile acid synthesis without primary malabsorption**, seen in hypertriglyceridemia and during metformin therapy for diabetes. ~20% of patients on metformin have accelerated bowel transit times; patients with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) show increased hepatic bile acid production, diarrhea, and elevated serum C4; obesity + hypertriglyceridemia can independently drive increased bile acid synthesis and idiopathic BAM. This is a genuinely distinct mechanism from Types 1-3 — worth knowing about since it implies metabolic status (triglycerides, metformin use) is itself a variable in BAM severity, separate from the ileal/TGR5 story.
Source: Di Ciaula, A. et al., "Advances in the pathophysiology, diagnosis and management of chronic diarrhoea from bile acid malabsorption: a systematic review," *Eur J Intern Med* 2024, 128:10–19 — https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ejim.2024.07.008 — **you provided this PDF directly; confirmed by full-text read.**

**[USER-PROVIDED PDF — VERIFIED, new — directly relevant to your own diagnostic history]** Diagnostic delay is the norm, not the exception: **up to one-half of BAM patients report symptoms lasting more than 5 years before receiving a correct diagnosis.** Your own multi-year path through the NHS before a private diagnosis is squarely consistent with this documented pattern, not an outlier case of being "mismanaged" — this appears to be how the disease is generally handled everywhere, not just in your specific case.
Source: Di Ciaula et al. 2024, citing Bannaga et al., "How bad is bile acid diarrhoea: an online survey of patient-reported symptoms and outcomes," *BMJ Open Gastroenterol* 2017.

**[USER-PROVIDED PDF — VERIFIED, new — formal meta-analysis, higher evidence tier than a single cohort study]** Slattery et al. 2015 (*Aliment Pharmacol Ther*) is a **systematic review with meta-analysis** (not just a narrative review) of BAM prevalence specifically in IBS-D patients meeting accepted diagnostic criteria (Manning/Kruis/Rome I/II/III). Pooling 6 studies (908 individuals total, SeHCAT retention <10% at 7 days as the BAM definition): individual study rates ranged 16.9%–35.3%, crude pooled rate 29.3%, and the formal random-effects **pooled rate was 28.1% (95% CI 22.6–34%)**. Neither the diagnostic criteria used nor the study country significantly modified this effect (i.e., the ~28% figure is robust across different IBS-D definitions and appears in both UK and Swedish cohorts) — though there was significant statistical heterogeneity across studies (I²=72.1%) not explained by the pre-specified subgroup analyses. Study quality was rated high overall (via QUADAS-2), though 4 of 6 studies were from tertiary care centres, limiting generalizability to primary/general care populations.
> "These data provide evidence that in excess of one quarter of patients meeting accepted criteria for IBS-D have BAM. This distinction has implications for the interpretation of previous studies as well as contemporaneous clinical practice and future guideline development."
Source: Slattery, S.A.; Niaz, O.; Aziz, Q.; Ford, A.C.; Farmer, A.D. "Systematic review with meta-analysis: the prevalence of bile acid malabsorption in the irritable bowel syndrome with diarrhoea." *Aliment Pharmacol Ther* 2015, 42(1):3–11 — https://doi.org/10.1111/apt.13227 — **you provided this PDF directly; confirmed by full-text read.**

**[USER-PROVIDED PDF — VERIFIED, new]** Diagnostic test performance data, now with two independently-read reviews largely agreeing on the numbers:
- **SeHCAT**: 94% sensitivity, 100% specificity vs. healthy subjects; 87.3% sensitivity, 93.2% specificity when compared against serum C4 (which itself had 85.2%/71.1% in that same comparison). Widely regarded as the single most accurate test, with accuracy increasing further at the <10% retention cutoff specifically (vs. <15%).
- **48-hour fecal bile acid test**: cutoffs are total fecal bile acids ≥2337 μmol/48h, OR primary bile acids >10% of total, OR total ≥1000 μmol/48h combined with primary bile acids >4%. Sensitivity/specificity for detecting SeHCAT<15%+primary BA>10% was only 45%/63% in one study — a meaningfully weaker test than SeHCAT alone.
- **Serum C4**: 82.6% sensitivity, 84.3% specificity for detecting BAM generally; at a cutoff of >48.4 ng/mL, 90% sensitivity/79% specificity vs. SeHCAT<10%; NPV 98%/PPV 74% in another comparison. Combining C4 with ≥1.1 daily watery (Bristol type 6-7) stools improved this to 70% sensitivity/95% specificity, and combining C4 + total stool bile acids + watery-stool frequency improved further to 77% sensitivity/93% specificity.
- **Serum FGF19**: at <145 pg/mL, 58% sensitivity/84% specificity; at <60 ng/L specifically in Crohn's-ileitis-related diarrhea, 80% sensitivity/68% specificity — FGF19 alone is the weakest single biomarker, consistent with the Yang 2024 review's finding of only 29% sensitivity when used alone.
- **UK-specific access data**: SeHCAT is only available in 30-40% of UK GI centres — directly explaining why a private gastroenterologist may have been necessary to get definitively diagnosed, and consistent with your own experience of NHS delay before paying for private care.
Source: Di Ciaula et al. 2024, Table 2 (diagnostic accuracy) and main text — user-provided PDF, verified by full-text read.

## 2. Long-Term Sequestrant Treatment Trajectories (colesevelam, cholestyramine, colestipol)

**[VERIFIED]** In a retrospective cohort of 282 microscopic-colitis patients treated with bile acid sequestrants (median follow-up **4.5 years**), outcomes were: 49.3% complete response, 16.3% partial response, 24.8% non-response, 9.6% intolerance.
> "Clinical outcomes were: 49.3% complete response, 16.3% partial response, 24.8% non-response, and 9.6% intolerance. The study identified 282 patients (median age, 59 years; 88.3% women) with median follow-up of 4.5 years, treated with cholestyramine (64.9%), colesevelam (21.6%), and colestipol (13.5%)."
Source: https://www.cghjournal.org/article/S1542-3565(23)00331-2/fulltext (vote 3-0)
*(Note: this cohort is microscopic colitis, not primary BAM, but sequestrant response-durability data of this kind is scarce — most directly relevant real-world dataset found.)*

**[VERIFIED]** Among the 9.6% intolerant to sequestrants, the most common adverse reactions were dyspepsia (44.4%) and nausea (33.3%), with headache (14.8%), myalgia and rash (3.7% each) less frequent.
> "Patients intolerant to bile acid sequestrants had the following adverse reactions: dyspepsia (12; 44.4%), nausea (9; 33.3%), headache (4; 14.8%), myalgia (1; 3.7%), and rash (1; 3.7%)."
Source: same CGH cohort (vote 3-0)

**[VERIFIED]** A separate retrospective chart review + patient questionnaire of 45 cancer patients (37F/8M, median age 58) used colesevelam for BAM caused by pelvic radiotherapy (n=29), small-bowel resection (12), upper-GI surgery (2), high-dose chemo (1), or new-onset Crohn's (1).
> "In total, 45 patients... who received treatment with colesevelam between 2004 and 2007 were included. Identifiable causes of bile acid malabsorption in this sample population were pelvic radiotherapy (n = 29), small-bowel resection (12), upper gastrointestinal surgery (2), high-dose chemotherapy (1), and new-onset Crohn's disease (1)."
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20109999/ (vote 3-0)

**[VERIFIED]** As of that 2010 paper's publication, no prospective controlled-trial evidence yet existed for colesevelam in BAM — only retrospective/questionnaire data — and the authors explicitly called for RCTs.
> "In view of the data found in this retrospective chart review and patient questionnaire, prospective, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials of colesevelam for bile acid malabsorption are warranted."
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20109999/ (vote 3-0)

**[USER-PROVIDED PDF — VERIFIED, new — this gap is now filled]** That RCT now exists: **Borup et al. 2023, *Lancet Gastroenterology & Hepatology*** — a double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled **Phase 4 clinical trial** found colesevelam superior to placebo at inducing response in BAD diagnosed with C4 concentration >46 ng/mL. This is a genuinely higher-quality evidence tier than everything else in this section (RCT vs. retrospective/questionnaire data).
Source: Yang et al. 2024, citing Borup, C. et al., "Efficacy and Safety of Colesevelam for the Treatment of Bile Acid Diarrhoea: A Double-Blind, Randomised, Placebo-Controlled, Phase 4 Clinical Trial," *Lancet Gastroenterol Hepatol* 2023, 8:321–331 — https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-1253(22)00401-0

**[USER-PROVIDED PDF — VERIFIED — now upgraded from a secondhand Yang citation to the actual primary trial, read in full]** **Vijayvargiya et al. 2020, *Clin Gastroenterol Hepatol* 18(13):2962–2970.e6** — a double-blind, parallel-group, placebo-controlled 28-day RCT (n=30 adults, IBS-D with documented increased BA synthesis/excretion, single center, Dec 2017–Dec 2018) of **colesevelam 1875 mg (3 × 625 mg tablets) twice daily** vs matching placebo. Verified findings:
- **Colesevelam measurably remodels the colonic BA-signalling machinery, not just luminal binding**: vs placebo, it significantly **increased sequestered fecal total BA excretion (P<0.001)**, **increased serum C4 (BA synthesis)** and **decreased serum FGF19 (both P<0.001)** — i.e., by mopping up luminal BA it releases the FXR→FGF19 brake and *upregulates* hepatic BA synthesis (the body fighting the sequestrant).
- **The direct gene-expression result (rectosigmoid biopsies, post/pre ratio per individual)**: colesevelam **reduced mucosal expression of NR1H4 (the FXR gene)** and P2RY4, and **increased expression of GPBAR1 (= TGR5)** — plus upregulated the inflammation-related gene **CCL20**. So your medication is not pharmacologically inert at the colonic wall: it shifts the very FXR/TGR5 receptors that Section 6 ties to the MDMA discussion, and (notably) *raises* colonic TGR5 expression.
- **But symptoms/transit didn't move**: no significant between-group difference in stool frequency, stool consistency, colonic transit (scintigraphy), or mucosal permeability (¹³C-mannitol). Well tolerated. (Consistent with the aldafermin pattern above — biochemistry/receptor changes ≠ symptom change in these small short trials.)

Note the trial dose (1875 mg **twice** daily = 3.75 g/day) is well above your current tapered "2 pills once daily" — a useful contrast for your own dose-response question. Citation: Vijayvargiya et al. 2020, *Clin Gastroenterol Hepatol* 18(13):2962–2970.e6, doi:10.1016/j.cgh.2020.02.027, PMID 32088296, PMC7442687 — user-provided PDF (`nihms1568739`), verified by full-text read.

**[USER-PROVIDED PDF — VERIFIED — now upgraded from a secondhand Yang citation to the actual BouSaba 2022 primary paper, read in full]** **BouSaba et al. 2022, *Clin Gastroenterol Hepatol* 20(9):2083–2090.e1** — a Mayo observational study of **219 IBS-D patients (79% female)**, of whom **44 had BAD** (defined by fasting serum C4 >52.5 ng/mL). Verified findings:
- **BAD-within-IBS-D is a distinctly worse phenotype**: the BAD subgroup was significantly **older**, had **higher BMI (33.3 vs 29.2 kg/m², P<0.001)**, more prior cholecystectomy, more severe bowel dysfunction, and worse IBS-specific QoL (the "need to be near a toilet" domain, P=0.0105) than IBS-D patients *without* BAD.
- **Symptom predictors of BAD (multivariable logistic model, AUC 0.69)**: frequency of loose bowel movements (**OR 1.35, 95% CI 1.08–1.68**) and worrying about losing bowel control (**OR 1.42, 95% CI 1.08–1.86**); severity of diarrhea + toilet proximity were significant predictors (P<0.01). Concretely, **80.4% of BAD patients reported an urgent need to defecate >25% of the time.**
- **Depression signal**: using a HAD depression subscore cutoff >8, **9.1% of BAD patients screened positive vs only 1.9% of non-BAD IBS-D** — i.e., the depression association is with the *bile-acid* subtype specifically, not IBS-D generally. BAD patients also took significantly more bile acid binders (P=0.0043), antidiarrheals, and antacid secretory agents.

Directly relevant to your "not too bad to alright" baseline and brain-fog: this is human data that the BAD subtype carries a heavier somatic + mood burden than ordinary IBS-D, and that the burden tracks with the diarrhea/urgency severity rather than being purely psychological. Citation: BouSaba et al. 2022, *Clin Gastroenterol Hepatol* 20(9):2083–2090.e1, doi:10.1016/j.cgh.2021.11.035, PMID 34871814 — user-provided PDF (`PIIS1542356521012738`), verified by full-text read.

**[USER-PROVIDED PDF — VERIFIED, new — a genuinely new treatment avenue worth knowing about, not previously in this dossier]** A GLP-1 receptor agonist (**liraglutide**) has been directly compared to colesevelam in a randomized, double-blind, double-dummy, non-inferiority clinical trial in BAM patients diagnosed by SeHCAT. Liraglutide (uptitrated 0.6–1.8mg/day subcutaneous over 3 weeks) showed a **significant risk difference of −27% in favour of liraglutide** for reducing daily stool frequency compared to colesevelam (3 capsules of 625mg twice daily). Proposed mechanism: liraglutide likely enhances passive/active bile acid reabsorption in the small intestine, reducing colonic bile acid spillover (rather than binding bile acids like a sequestrant does).
> "Results showed a significant risk difference of –27% in favour of liraglutide in reducing daily stool frequency."
**This is worth raising with your gastroenterologist directly** — if your current colesevelam+ondansetron regimen has plateaued at "not too bad to alright" rather than resolving fully, liraglutide represents a mechanistically distinct, RCT-validated alternative/adjunct that doesn't appear to have come up in your treatment history so far (a GLP-1 agonist, the same drug class as semaglutide/Ozempic, repurposed for a completely different mechanism here).

**[USER-PROVIDED PDF — VERIFIED — the Merza network meta-analysis, now read in full (open-access)]** **Merza et al. 2024, *J Clin Med Res* 16(2-3):33–45** — a frequentist network meta-analysis of **7 RCTs, 213 participants** (~age 50), ranking BAM treatments. Verified head-to-head results:
- **Tropifexor (FXR agonist) ranked #1 overall** — most effective at *raising* FGF19 (MD +335.30, 95% CI 334.86–335.74 vs placebo) and *lowering* C4 (MD −24.60, 95% CI −25.37 to −23.83). I.e., it best corrects the underlying biochemistry.
- **Liraglutide ranked #1 for reducing fecal bile acid concentration** (MD −19, 95% CI −37.61 to −0.39 vs colesevelam/placebo) — the one drug that most directly lowers the actual colonic BA load driving your symptoms.
- **Important scope caveat**: the title specifies **Inflammatory Bowel Disease patients** — this is a BAM-in-IBD population, and the authors explicitly flag the small pool (213) and call for larger RCTs. So the ranking is suggestive, not definitive, and its IBD framing means it maps only loosely onto idiopathic/Type-2 BAM. Citation: Merza et al. 2024, *J Clin Med Res* 16(2-3):33–45, doi:10.14740/jocmr5007, PMID 38550552, PMC10970039 — user-provided PDF (`jocmr16033`), verified.

**[USER-PROVIDED PDF — VERIFIED — the mechanistic companion to the liraglutide-vs-colesevelam RCT, open-access, read in full]** **Ellegaard, Kårhus et al. 2024, *Clin Transl Gastroenterol*** ("Liraglutide and Colesevelam Change Serum and Fecal Bile Acid Levels in a Randomized Trial") — a secondary analysis of the same Kårhus 2022 RCT, showing the two drugs work through *distinct* bile-acid mechanisms:
- **Colesevelam**: increased fecal concentrations of *all* bile acid species (the sequestration you'd expect) **and** decreased serum secondary bile acids.
- **Liraglutide**: induced only a *small increase in serum unconjugated bile acids* with **no change in fecal bile acid concentration** — i.e., liraglutide does *not* work by dumping BA into stool; its benefit (fewer stools, per the parent RCT) comes from a different route (slowed transit / enhanced small-bowel handling), not luminal BA removal. This directly reconciles with the Merza finding above and sharpens the mechanistic contrast for your meta-analysis: two effective drugs, opposite effects on where the bile acids end up. Citation: Ellegaard, Kårhus et al. 2024, *Clin Transl Gastroenterol*, PMC11596762 — user-provided PDF (`ct915e00772`), verified.

**[USER-PROVIDED PDF — VERIFIED, new]** FXR agonists: **obeticholic acid (OCA)** increased FGF19 in both primary and secondary BAD over 2 weeks in a small open-label pilot study, was well tolerated with only minor adverse effects (mild headaches). **Tropifexor** (60μg once daily) was safe and well-tolerated in a double-blind multicenter RCT for primary BAD. The same network meta-analysis above found **tropifexor was the single most effective treatment for raising FGF19 and lowering C4 levels** (the underlying biochemical markers) compared to placebo — but note this is a biochemical-marker outcome, not necessarily symptom improvement, and FXR agonists are "hardly used in clinical practice" per this review, mainly due to side effects (itching with long-term OCA) and high costs.

**[USER-PROVIDED PDF — VERIFIED, new — population-level epidemiology, open-access]** **Kårhus, Ellegaard et al. 2023, *Clin Epidemiol* 15:1173–1181** ("The Epidemiology of Bile Acid Diarrhea in Denmark") — a national registry study, **5,264 BAD individuals identified 2003–2021** (ICD-10 K90.8B), each matched to 10 general-population controls (52,640). Verified findings directly relevant to your "how does this go over years" question:
- Estimated general-population BAD **prevalence 1–2%** — i.e., this is a common, under-diagnosed condition, not a rare one.
