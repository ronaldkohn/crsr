## Part 1 — What is actually wrong with you, in plain terms

### The one-paragraph version
Your liver makes **bile acids** — detergent-like molecules that get dumped into your small intestine to help you digest fat. Normally, ~95% of them are reabsorbed near the end of the small intestine (the terminal ileum) and recycled. In **bile acid malabsorption (BAM)**, too many bile acids escape that reabsorption and spill into your **colon**. There, they act like an irritant and a laxative: they pull water and salt into the bowel, speed everything up, make the gut lining more permeable and more sensitive, and can drive inflammation. The result is the exact cluster you had — urgent, watery diarrhea, bloating, pain, and (via mechanisms we'll get to) even brain fog.

### Why it's so hard to diagnose (and why you got kicked around for years)
This is the single most validating finding in the whole dossier, so it's worth stating clearly:

- **Diagnostic delay is the norm, not your bad luck.** Up to **half** of BAM patients report symptoms lasting **more than 5 years** before getting the right diagnosis. Your multi-year NHS odyssey is *the documented typical pattern*, not evidence you were uniquely mismanaged.
- **The best test is scarce.** The gold-standard test, **SeHCAT** (a nuclear-medicine scan that measures how much of a labeled bile acid your body retains over 7 days), is only available in an estimated **30–40% of UK gastroenterology centres.** That scarcity is a big structural reason people go undiagnosed — and a concrete reason a **private** gastroenterologist may have been what it took to get you a definitive answer.
- **It hides behind IBS.** BAM overlaps heavily with diarrhea-predominant IBS (IBS-D). In fact, formal meta-analysis found that **~28% of people diagnosed with IBS-D actually have BAM** — more than one in four. So the "process of elimination" your gastroenterologist ran (ruling out SIBO, ruling out IBS) is exactly the right way to find it, because BAM is what's *left* when you correctly exclude the mimics.

**What this means for you:** the years of no progress weren't a personal failure of your care so much as a systemic feature of a common-but-under-looked-for condition with a scarce test. That's not a comforting thing about the health system, but it should retire any lingering sense that *you* did something wrong or should have pushed harder.

### Which "type" of BAM you might have
The literature recognizes subtypes:
- **Type 1** — caused by disease or surgery at the terminal ileum (Crohn's, resection, radiation).
- **Type 2** — **idiopathic / primary** (no identifiable structural cause). This is the classic "it just started" form.
- **Type 3** — secondary to other conditions (celiac, gallbladder removal, etc.).
- **Type 4** (newer, 2024 literature) — driven by *over-production* of bile acids rather than poor reabsorption; linked to high triglycerides and to the diabetes drug metformin.

You described symptoms **starting after moving to London**, with no mention of ileal surgery, Crohn's, or gallbladder removal — which is most consistent with **Type 2 (primary/idiopathic)**, and possibly a **post-infectious** trigger (see below). But this dossier can't assign your subtype for you; that's a conversation with the gastroenterologist who has your actual SeHCAT/C4 numbers and history.

### The "developed an illness after moving to London" detail is potentially meaningful
The ondansetron trial literature notes that IBS-D — and by extension the serotonin-sensitive diarrheal picture you fit — is often **post-infective**: it can begin after a gut infection ("developed an illness after moving") and then persist, with lasting changes to the gut's serotonin-producing cells. This is worth mentioning to your doctor if you haven't, because a post-infectious onset is a recognized, coherent origin story that fits both your timeline and your strong response to a serotonin-targeting drug.

## Part 2 — What's happening inside your gut (the mechanism, made simple)

You don't need this section to manage your condition, but you asked "what's going on," and there genuinely *is* a satisfying mechanistic story. It's also the load-bearing part of the MDMA discussion, so it's worth understanding.

### The key player: a receptor called TGR5
Think of **TGR5** as a "bile acid detector" sitting on cells in your gut wall. When bile acids wash over it, it fires. In your colon, TGR5 sits on special cells (**enterochromaffin cells**, or EC cells) that respond by releasing **serotonin** — and yes, most of your body's serotonin lives in your gut, not your brain. That serotonin then triggers a nerve cascade (via a second messenger, CGRP) that tells the bowel to **contract and move things along**. This is the normal machinery of peristalsis — the wave that moves stool.

**Now put it together for BAM:** you have too many bile acids reaching the colon → they over-activate TGR5 → too much serotonin gets released → the gut contracts too hard and too fast → urgency, cramping, watery diarrhea. The field's own authority states your disease mechanism almost verbatim: *bile-acid diarrhea is essentially* **"over-activation of TGR5."**

This isn't hand-waving — it's confirmed in mouse studies where deleting the TGR5 gene made bowel movements 2.6× less frequent, and confirmed in **human colon biopsies**, where IBS-D patients had *elevated* TGR5 in the colon lining, and the more TGR5 they had, the *worse their pain.* Real human tissue, not just theory.

### The elegant twist: TGR5 has a brake as well as an accelerator
TGR5 also sits on a different set of nerves (inhibitory motor neurons) where activating it *slows* the gut down via nitric oxide. So TGR5 is really a two-way switch — pro-movement in one location, anti-movement in another. This matters because it explains why the same bile-acid signaling system can produce diarrhea in one context and constipation in another, and why the drugs that touch it have complicated, sometimes counterintuitive effects.

### Why this might explain your brain fog too
Here's a genuinely interesting thread the research surfaced. Gut serotonin can't cross into the brain directly — but it signals to the brain **through the vagus nerve**, and the bile-acid hormone system (via a molecule called FGF19) appears to tap into the **HPA axis**, your body's central stress-hormone system. So there's a plausible, published **gut → brain pathway** connecting a bile-acid-dysregulated gut to central symptoms like brain fog. No study proves "BAM causes brain fog" outright — treat this as *biologically plausible inference, not established fact* — but it's a real, citable mechanism, not a stretch. It also means your brain fog may be a genuine downstream feature of the gut condition, not a separate problem or "all in your head."

## Part 3 — Your treatment, and whether you've been doing it right (you have)

### Colesevelam — what it does and why you're on it
Colesevelam is a **bile acid sequestrant** — a molecular sponge that binds bile acids in your gut so they get carried out in stool instead of irritating your colon. It's the more refined cousin of the older drug cholestyramine, with **4–6× stronger binding** and notably better tolerability (fewer side effects, easier to take as a tablet vs. a gritty powder). Being put on colesevelam rather than cholestyramine was a reasonable, well-tolerated choice.

Your **starting dose** — "3 doses of 2 pills per day" — is the *exact* textbook BAD dose in the guideline ("two 625 mg tablets, three times per day"). So you started on a standard, by-the-book regimen.

**One subtlety worth knowing:** in a careful trial, colesevelam did something interesting at the level of your gut wall — by mopping up bile acids it actually *raised* colonic TGR5 expression and nudged the body to make *more* bile acids (the body "fighting back" against the sponge). Yet in that same short trial, symptoms and transit barely moved even though the biochemistry shifted a lot. This "biochemistry moves, symptoms lag" pattern shows up repeatedly across BAM drugs and is a useful reminder that lab numbers and how you *feel* don't always track together.

### Ondansetron — the clever adjunct
Ondansetron is normally an anti-nausea drug (a **5-HT3 serotonin-receptor blocker**). Using it for diarrhea is off-label but mechanistically smart: remember that in BAM the problem runs *through serotonin* (bile acids → TGR5 → serotonin → over-active gut). Ondansetron blunts the serotonin signal, and in the key trial it **slowed gut transit by ~10 hours**, pulling abnormally-fast IBS-D guts back toward normal, and — crucially — its **biggest benefit was on urgency**, one of the most life-limiting symptoms. Benefit appears within a week and fades within a week of stopping, which matches your sense of it being a fairly acute, tangible effect.

**Two honest caveats on ondansetron for your case:**
1. The main trials that prove it works **deliberately excluded BAM patients** — they studied IBS-D *without* bile acid malabsorption. So your doctor prescribing it *for* BAM is a reasonable mechanistic extrapolation (the serotonin mechanism is shared), but the trial evidence base is technically in a neighboring condition, not BAM itself.
2. There's a documented sub-group of **"super-responders"** who control symptoms on **under 4 mg/day** — and they tend to have a specific serotonin profile. Your ability to taper to a **single low daily dose** fits this super-responder pattern nicely.

### You tapered correctly — this is the formal recommendation, not a liberty you took
This is worth emphasizing because you framed your dose reduction almost apologetically. The authoritative guideline explicitly recommends:
- **"Maintenance therapy should be used at the lowest dose needed to minimize symptoms."**
- **"Intermittent, on-demand dosing"** should be tried in responders.

Your progression — from 3×2 colesevelam + 2 ondansetron down to 2 colesevelam + 1 ondansetron — is **exactly** this strategy executed well. You were following best practice, whether by design or by feel. Minimizing your dose also reduces long-term risks (see monitoring, below), so it's the *right* direction to have moved.

### How far can you taper? What the withdrawal data says
The honest answer is "it depends on your subtype and severity, and there's a real chance of relapse if you go too low." Two cohorts frame the range:
- In **post-gallbladder** BAM, when the drug was withdrawn, **61% stayed regular** on only occasional on-demand dosing — most could nearly stop.
- In a **mixed BAD + IBS-D** group, **94% relapsed** on withdrawal and only 6% could stop entirely.

So some people (usually milder/secondary) sustain control on almost nothing; most cannot fully stop. Your having plateaued at a low-but-nonzero dose that keeps you at "alright" is squarely consistent with this — you may have found roughly your floor. Also useful: **the more severe your original SeHCAT result, the more likely and complete your response** to the sequestrant tends to be.

## Part 4 — What to expect over the years (your original question)

You asked how people progress over years on this medication. The dossier assembled unusually direct evidence for this.

### The headline: it's a lifelong-management condition, and stability is the goal (not cure)
The clinical guideline states it plainly: **"BAD generally is not cured, and… many patients will require lifelong treatment."** So the correct mental model isn't "am I getting better or worse?" but "am I stably managed?" By that measure — years in, on a low dose, at "not too bad to alright" — **you are doing well.** "Improved but not 100%, still needing some medication" is the *documented normal destination*, not a warning sign.

### The medication's own risk profile is *not* flat over years — there's a two-phase pattern
A large 20-year, 5,286-report safety analysis (the most directly relevant study to your "over the years" question) found a **biphasic risk pattern** for bile acid sequestrants:
- **~Half of adverse events cluster in the first month** (an early adaptation period), then
- **A second rise after ~6 months of continuous use**, with reports persisting past a year — attributed to slow-building effects: fat-soluble vitamin deficiencies, electrolyte shifts, and changes to gut bacteria.

For **colesevelam specifically**, that analysis flagged a distinctive **muscle signature** — signals for **myalgia (muscle aches) and muscle spasms** not listed on the label — hypothesized to come from mineral/vitamin-D issues due to long-term bile-acid binding. (File that away — it's a mildly interesting counterpoint to your MDMA *muscle* observation, though they're unrelated mechanisms.) It also flagged reflux and, rarely, hardened stool (fecaloma) from the drying effect of bile-acid binding.

**What this means practically:** none of this is alarming, but it's the concrete argument *for* your instinct to keep the dose as low as it can go, and *for* a periodic check-in on the monitoring items below.

### If your symptoms ever clearly worsen on a stable dose — that's a specific flag
The guideline says worsening symptoms despite stable treatment should prompt **re-evaluation**, because the differential includes things like microscopic colitis, Crohn's, celiac, or SIBO layering on top. So stable-but-imperfect = expected; a clear, sustained worsening = worth a fresh look, not just a dose bump.

## Part 5 — The MDMA observation: taken seriously, honestly

You described something specific and reproducible: an abrupt need to empty your bowels, then bloating vanishing, posture straightening, and your abdominal muscles becoming able to contract "like stone" again — plus a general sense of physical relief that you're careful to distinguish from the mood effect. You suspect a real anti-inflammatory or gut-signaling mechanism. Here's the honest, evidence-graded read.

### First, the crucial honesty: nobody has written this up
Across **two separate dedicated search passes with many phrasings**, the research found **zero** documented case reports, forum posts, or studies of a BAM/IBS/IBD patient getting *gut relief* from MDMA. Worse for the hypothesis, the *general* MDMA literature points the opposite way — MDMA is typically described as *disrupting* digestion (urgency, loose stools) via a fight-or-flight, blood-shunted-away-from-the-gut mechanism. So the mainstream expectation runs **counter** to what you report.

That's not a refutation — individual physiology in a *bile-acid-diseased* gut can differ from a healthy one, and forums (Reddit/Erowid) are poorly indexed by search, so absence of evidence isn't strong evidence of absence. But it means your experience, if accurate, is **genuinely distinctive and undocumented.** Keep that framing: it makes your observation *more* interesting to investigate, not something already validated.

### Now, why it's mechanistically plausible anyway — three verified threads that converge
Here's what makes your report worth taking seriously rather than dismissing. Several *independently verified* facts line up:

1. **MDMA genuinely is anti-inflammatory — this is real and well-documented.** In controlled studies MDMA suppresses the inflammatory signals **TNF-α and IL-1β** and pushes the immune system toward an anti-inflammatory state (more IL-10, more TGF-β). The TNF-α suppression is the most robust and longest-lasting effect. And critically, it's **not** a direct drug-on-immune-cell effect — it works *indirectly*, through your stress-hormone (HPA) axis and stress chemicals (cortisol, catecholamines). This is the single strongest support for your "it's physiological, not just mood" intuition: the anti-inflammatory action is real, systemic, and whole-body — exactly how you describe it feeling.

2. **Your own bile-acid/TGR5 system *also* suppresses TNF-α.** Two separate reviews confirm that bile-acid signaling (through both TGR5 and the related FXR receptor) suppresses the *same* inflammatory molecule, TNF-α, and dampens the same master inflammatory switch (NF-κB). So you have **two arms of your situation — bile-acid signaling and MDMA — converging on the same anti-inflammatory endpoint.** That's a real, doubly-sourced convergence. (Important caveat: shared *endpoint* is not proof of shared *pathway* — TGR5 does it locally in gut immune cells, MDMA does it systemically via stress hormones. They arrive at the same place by different roads.)

3. **The whole system is serotonin-based, and MDMA is a serotonin flood.** Your gut's malfunction runs through serotonin (bile acids → TGR5 → serotonin → motility). MDMA's primary action is a massive release of serotonin. It's entirely coherent that dumping a large serotonin signal into a serotonin-sensitized gut produces a dramatic, abrupt effect — plausibly the "empty the bowels *then* feel relief" sequence you describe (an acute serotonergic surge/purge, followed by the anti-inflammatory calm).

### The muscle / "abs like stone" part — partially explained
This surprised the research in a good way. MDMA's **single most common physical effect** in the Phase 3 trials was **"muscle tightness" (58.5% of participants)** — and importantly, the trials track this **separately** from jaw-clenching/bruxism (13.2%). So there *is* a documented, distinct, whole-body muscle-tightening effect of MDMA that isn't just the stereotyped jaw thing. What no trial does is *localize* it to a body region — there's no "abdominal tightness" data point. So the accurate read is: your "abs turn to stone" is **plausibly a regional expression of MDMA's well-documented general muscle-tightness effect**, not something wholly unheard of. The specific *abdominal-predominant* pattern remains uncharacterized in the literature — which again makes your observation a distinctive data point rather than a contradiction.

(One honest alternative to hold in mind: some of the acute gut effect — the urgency and loose stool — is, per pharmacology, simply an *expected low-grade serotonergic side effect of MDMA itself*, on the same spectrum as its other effects. So part of what you feel may be "MDMA doing MDMA things to any gut," and part may be the genuinely-anti-inflammatory relief that follows. Both can be true at once.)

### The bottom line on MDMA
- **Real and supported:** MDMA suppresses TNF-α and is systemically anti-inflammatory; your gut disease is serotonin/TGR5-based; the two converge on the same inflammatory endpoint; MDMA has a documented whole-body muscle-tightness effect.
- **Your synthesis (plausible, unproven):** that *these* mechanisms are *why your specific gut* feels dramatically better — an inference bridging separately-established facts, not something any single study shows.
- **Undocumented:** no one else's matching account exists in searchable literature.
- **The framing the source authors would insist on:** the immunology literature calls MDMA's effect **immunosuppression** and frames it as a *risk* (infection susceptibility with repeated use), not a therapy. "MDMA suppresses TNF-α" is solid; "MDMA is a good anti-inflammatory treatment for a gut condition" is your hypothesis extrapolating from that, and the same authors would wave a caution flag at the leap.

### Safety — the part that isn't optional
Because this is a real drug interaction question, the report has to be straight with you:

- **Your existing protocol is the smart one.** You already **never take ondansetron on an MDMA day** to avoid stacking two serotonin drugs. That's the conservative, correct call — it costs you nothing and removes a variable. Keep doing exactly that.
- **The serotonin-syndrome picture, accurately:** In 2014 the FDA formally warned that ondansetron (and its class) can contribute to **serotonin syndrome**. A pharmacologist's rebuttal argues 5-HT3 blockers like ondansetron don't meaningfully add serotonin-toxicity risk on their own — and the real-world data backs a nuance: essentially **every documented serotonin-syndrome case involved the 5-HT3 drug *plus another serotonergic drug*.** But here's the point that matters for you: **MDMA is itself a potent serotonergic drug.** So the genuinely risky pattern — combining serotonergic agents — is one MDMA *already participates in*. The specific MDMA + ondansetron combination has never been studied, which means "not shown to be dangerous" is **not** the same as "shown to be safe." Your instinct to keep them apart is well-founded.
- **The trial safety data has a blind spot that's exactly your use case.** MDMA's trial safety record (no serious lasting harm, effects resolving within ~7 days) covers **acute, supervised, single doses only.** The meta-analysis is explicit that there is **essentially no data on repeated, long-term, real-world-pattern use** — which is the pattern relevant to you. So the reassuring safety numbers don't actually speak to your situation.
- **The unglamorous risks are real:** MDMA raises body temperature, and its immune-suppressing effect (the very thing you may be benefiting from) is framed in the literature as increasing **infection susceptibility** with repeated use. This is a real trade-off, not a free lunch.

None of this is a lecture to stop — you're an adult making informed choices and you've clearly already thought about the interaction. It's to make sure the *safety* half of the picture is as evidence-based as the *interesting* half.

## Part 6 — Things to consider (all "discuss with your doctor," none "do this now")

These are the concrete, actionable threads the research turned up. Prioritized.

### 1. There may be a meaningfully better drug for you: liraglutide
This is the single most actionable finding, and it doesn't appear to have come up in your treatment history. **Liraglutide** — a **GLP-1 agonist**, the same drug *class* as Ozempic/semaglutide, but repurposed here for a completely different mechanism — was compared **head-to-head against colesevelam** in a randomized trial in BAM patients. Result: a **27% advantage for liraglutide** in reducing daily stool frequency, and in a separate network meta-analysis it was **the single most effective treatment for lowering the actual colonic bile-acid load** driving symptoms. It works differently from colesevelam (it doesn't sponge up bile acids into your stool; it seems to improve how your small intestine handles them and slows transit).

**Why raise it:** if your current regimen has plateaued at "alright" rather than "good," a mechanistically distinct, RCT-validated alternative or add-on exists that your gastroenterologist can evaluate. (Caveats: the trials were small and single-centre; the meta-analysis was in an IBD-BAM population, so it maps only loosely onto primary/Type-2 BAM; and it's an injectable with mild nausea as a common side effect.) This is a "**bring the paper to your appointment**" item — Kårhus et al. 2022, *Lancet Gastroenterol Hepatol*.

There's also a neat conceptual convergence here worth mentioning to a curious clinician: **your current drug, colesevelam, is itself known to work partly through the TGR5 → GLP-1 pathway** — and liraglutide is a GLP-1 agonist. So your existing drug and the most promising alternative both converge on the same GLP-1 arm of bile-acid biology. That's not a reason to switch by itself, but it's an elegant sign the GLP-1 axis is a real lever in your specific condition.

### 2. Diet: a low-fat diet is a proven, drug-free lever
Bile is released in response to dietary fat, so **fat intake directly drives BAM severity.** The guideline notes a **low-fat diet (roughly <30 g fat/day, or <20% of calories) can improve symptoms** independently of medication. If you haven't systematically tried this, it's a no-downside experiment fully within your control — and it may let you hold your current low medication dose more comfortably, or explain day-to-day fluctuations you've noticed.

### 3. Drug timing: your ondansetron and colesevelam may be fighting each other
Colesevelam is a *sponge* — it can bind **other drugs** in your gut and reduce their absorption. Guidance is to take other medications **at least 1 hour before, or 4–6 hours after**, the sequestrant. **This is directly relevant to you:** if you take your ondansetron and colesevelam at the same time, the colesevelam may be **partially binding your ondansetron and blunting its effect.** Separating them by a few hours is a free, zero-risk experiment that could get you *more* out of your existing ondansetron dose. Worth a quick check with your pharmacist. (Colesevelam causes fewer such interactions than the older cholestyramine, so this is a "possible optimization," not a certainty — but it's easy to test.)

### 4. Long-term monitoring: a light touch, but worth a periodic look
Long-term bile-acid binding can, in theory, reduce absorption of **fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K)**. The reassuring news specific to *your* drug: **colesevelam was NOT associated with meaningful reductions in vitamins A, D, E, or K in studies up to a year** — another point in its favour over cholestyramine. Experts couldn't agree on routine annual vitamin testing, but suggested **periodic clotting checks (INR/prothrombin time)** may be prudent on long-term therapy. The colesevelam-specific safety analysis also raised (unlisted, hypothesis-level) signals for **muscle aches/spasms** tied to possible mineral/vitamin-D issues, and rare reflux/hardened-stool effects. Net: nothing to worry about acutely, but a **periodic check of vitamin D / minerals / clotting** on a multi-year course is a reasonable, low-effort safeguard to mention.

### 5. If symptoms clearly worsen on a stable dose — get re-evaluated, don't just self-escalate
Per the guideline, a sustained worsening despite stable dosing warrants a **fresh diagnostic look** (microscopic colitis, Crohn's, celiac, SIBO can co-occur). Stable-but-imperfect is expected and fine; a clear downward change is a specific reason to check back in.

## Part 7 — The honest gaps (what nobody actually knows)

Good synthesis names its own limits. Two real gaps sit at the center of your case, and both are *unusually interesting precisely because they're open*:

1. **No paper directly connects ondansetron/5-HT3 blockade to the bile-acid → TGR5 → serotonin loop specifically.** Everyone documents the two halves — the bile-acid-to-serotonin machinery, and 5-HT3 blockers helping IBS-D — but **nobody has joined them into a single causal chain** where ondansetron interrupts the *bile-acid-triggered* serotonin step. That missing link is *exactly* the bridge that would most cleanly explain why your bile-acid-driven disease responds so well to a serotonin blocker. The field treats these as parallel facts; connecting them is your own well-motivated synthesis, not an established citation. (This held up even after full reads of five separate reviews covering every component.)

2. **No documented account of a BAM/IBS/IBD patient getting gut relief from MDMA exists.** Your observation appears genuinely unwritten. That makes it either a personal idiosyncrasy or a real, unstudied phenomenon — and there's no way to tell which from the literature, because the literature is silent.

Both gaps mean the most *interesting* parts of your situation — the ondansetron-BAM mechanism and the MDMA-gut effect — are supported by coherent, converging biology but *not* by a direct study. That's the honest status: **plausible and well-reasoned, not proven.**
